\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Kafka exactly-once and stream-processing fault tolerance}
\label{sec:rel-eos}

Kafka's storage layer is a replicated, partitioned commit log with per-partition
ordering~\cite{kafka2015}. Its transactional API (KIP-98) extends this log with a transaction
coordinator, a producer epoch and a transaction log so that a producer can atomically append
records across partitions and commit consumer offsets as part of the same
transaction~\cite{kip98,kafka-design}. The guarantee is explicitly producer-scoped: the
transaction defines atomicity over Kafka's own durable state, not over arbitrary external
effects~\cite{kip98}. Two further design changes shape the failure behaviour we study: fencing by
\code{transactional.id} (KIP-447) lets the coordinator invalidate zombie producers after a
restart~\cite{kip447}, and incremental cooperative rebalancing (KIP-429) changes when and how
often offsets are committed during a rebalance~\cite{kip429}. Kafka Streams exposes these
guarantees as an opt-in processing guarantee, with at-least-once as the default~\cite{kafka-streams}.

Stream-processing systems reached the same architectural conclusion earlier: fault tolerance is
built around consistent snapshots of \emph{internal} state. MillWheel places timers and state
under its own fault-tolerance envelope~\cite{millwheel2013}; Flink's asynchronous snapshot
mechanism gives lightweight exactly-once state recovery without stopping the
pipeline~\cite{flink2017}; Spark Streaming recovers lineage-based state~\cite{dstreams2013}; and
timely dataflow coordinates by logical timestamps~\cite{naiad2013}. The theoretical basis is the
distributed snapshot algorithm of Chandy and Lamport~\cite{chandy1985}, and the stream/table
duality of Sax et al.\ frames a materialised view as a stream of updates over an evolving
table~\cite{sax2018}. Dataflow's model makes explicit the trade-off between correctness, latency
and cost~\cite{dataflow2015}. None of these frameworks makes an unrelated external database commit
part of its snapshot; the external sink is outside the envelope, which is precisely the boundary
this paper measures.

\subsection{Transactions, idempotency and consistency}
\label{sec:rel-txn}

Across two sites, atomic commitment requires a protocol. Two-phase commit blocks if its
coordinator fails, which is why consensus-based commit and compensation-based
approaches exist~\cite{txncommit2006,pg-prepare}. Sagas replace atomic commit with a sequence of
local transactions and compensating actions for long-lived work~\cite{sagas1987}; they restore
eventual consistency of a business process but do not give a duplicate-free ledger, because
compensations are observable intermediate states~\cite{saga2022}. Isolation levels are defined by
the anomalies they permit, and several ANSI levels allow exactly the lost-update and
dirty-read phenomena that would make an independently committed ledger row
unreliable~\cite{berenson1995,pg-isolation}. Coordination can be avoided only when application
invariants are confluent, i.e.\ do not require ordering between conflicting
operations~\cite{bailis2014}; the ledger's one-entry-per-event requirement is not confluent with
an at-least-once source, which is why the mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} must add
either idempotency, atomic progress or both. Optimistic concurrency and large-scale reliability
engineering supply the classical tools for the trade-off~\cite{kung1981,vogels2009}.

Idempotency is the second classical answer. Exactly-once delivery has been studied in message
middleware~\cite{eosmsg2001,eosmid2012}, and request identifiers that survive crashes are the
standard CORBA-era mechanism for deduplicating retried
invocations~\cite{eoscorba1999}. Modern APIs expose the same idea as idempotency
keys~\cite{stripe-idempotency,aws-idempotency}, and relational engines provide the enforcement
primitive as a unique constraint with \code{ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING}~\cite{pg-onconflict}. The
subtlety our paper isolates is that deduplication and loss are different failures: an idempotency
key makes a retry harmless but cannot restore an effect that was never written before progress
advanced.

\subsection{Microservice data patterns and the outbox}
\label{sec:rel-micro}

The transactional outbox publishes database changes safely by writing the outgoing message in the
same local transaction as the state change and relaying it afterwards~\cite{debezium-outbox};
event sourcing and CQRS build the application from an ordered event log instead of mutable
state~\cite{eventsourcing2022}; saga frameworks supply compensation
orchestration~\cite{saga2019,saga2022}. Surveys of microservice data management document the
pattern landscape and its open problems, including the absence of a shared definition of
consistency in event-driven systems, but they report no fault-injection
measurements~\cite{laigner2021,micro2026}. Our M2 is the consumer-side dual of the outbox: an
inbox claim plus an outbox publication log inside the ledger transaction, with the relay making
publication recoverable. We measure its cost and its residual exposure rather than assuming it.

\subsection{Double-entry ledgers, append-only state and logical order}
\label{sec:rel-ledger}

Double-entry bookkeeping is a six-century-old invariant-bearing model: every entry has equal
debits and credits, and the ledger is append-only. Triple-entry accounting extends it with an
externally auditable record of each entry~\cite{triple2019,triple2021}. The systems substrate for
such an append-only record is the log-structured store, whose append-only discipline speeds
recovery and bounds corruption~\cite{lsfs1992}, together with logical clocks that totally order
events in a distributed system~\cite{lamport1978}. Our ledger model in
Section~\ref{sec:model} is deliberately small --- one currency, balanced two-leg entries,
append-only postings --- but it is faithful to the invariants that distinguish a ledger from a
generic table, and the oracle tests them directly.

\subsection{Formal specification and verification}
\label{sec:rel-formal}

We use TLA+ to state the crash-point semantics and machine-check a bounded version of the
invariant-preservation claims~\cite{lamport1994}; Alloy's automatic analysis of structural
constraints is a complementary approach for data-model reasoning~\cite{jackson2002}. Verified
distributed systems make the fault model explicit and prove properties against it, most
prominently in the Verdi framework~\cite{verdi2015}. We follow that discipline: the assumptions
(crash-recovery faults, no Byzantine failures, a fixed database isolation level, a single host
clock) are stated in Section~\ref{sec:model}, and the TLC check in Section~\ref{sec:formal} is
reported with its bounds so that it cannot be mistaken for an unbounded proof.

\subsection{Measurement, fault injection and statistics}
\label{sec:rel-method}

Benchmarking fault-tolerant data systems requires methodological care. Hoefler and Belli survey
sound benchmarking and reporting practice~\cite{hoefler2015}; Raasveldt et al.\ catalogue recurring
fairness pitfalls in database performance testing~\cite{raasveldt2018}; Mytkowicz et al.\ show that
measurement bias alone can reverse performance conclusions~\cite{mytkowicz2009}; Dean and
Barroso explain why tail latency, not the mean, dominates responsiveness~\cite{dean2013}; and
Theodolite demonstrates use-case-based scalability benchmarking for stream
processors~\cite{theodolite2021}. We therefore fix all non-mechanism configuration identically,
randomise and interleave run order, use paired seeds, and report open-loop latency
distributions with HdrHistogram~\cite{hdrhistogram}.

Fault injection is our independent variable. Chaos engineering frames hypothesis-driven fault
experiments against a steady state~\cite{basiri2016}; black-box history checking can expose
isolation anomalies without instrumenting the database~\cite{elle2020}; deterministic simulation
tests every feature under many faults~\cite{fdb2021}; and bounded crash testing found bugs even in
verified systems~\cite{crashmonkey2019}. Artifact repeatability requires archived data and
incentive alignment~\cite{collberg2016}. Our injectors are scripted against real processes and real
TCP links (Toxiproxy~\cite{toxiproxy}) and write a timestamped receipt when they fire, so a run in
which the fault did not fire is excluded rather than silently counted as evidence.

Finally, our analysis plan follows the experimental-statistics literature: chance-affected
computational experiments need appropriate tests~\cite{arcuri2011,arcuri2012}; multiple
comparisons call for Friedman and Nemenyi procedures with exact post-hoc tests~\cite{demsar2006};
paired proportions use McNemar's test~\cite{mcnemar1947} with Holm's sequentially rejective
correction~\cite{holland1987}; effect sizes use Cliff's delta~\cite{cliff1993}; interval estimates
use the bootstrap~\cite{efron1979}; binomial intervals for mostly-zero violation counts use
Clopper--Pearson constructions~\cite{jsta2014,brown2001} and the rule of three for zero-event
bounds~\cite{rule3}.

\subsection{Research gap}
\label{sec:rel-gap}

The literature establishes three facts: Kafka EOS is bounded to Kafka's own state; external-state
correctness requires an explicit mechanism; and ledger invariants are unforgiving of duplicates
and losses. What is missing is an empirical, mechanism-level mapping from failure modes to ledger
invariant violations, with an independent oracle and the cost of each remedy measured under
identical conditions. The closest practitioner-facing statements are vendor
documentation~\cite{kafka-design,debezium-outbox,stripe-idempotency} and microservice
surveys~\cite{laigner2021,micro2026}, neither of which measures invariant violations under
injected faults. This paper provides that mapping, its formal basis, and a decision procedure for
practitioners.
